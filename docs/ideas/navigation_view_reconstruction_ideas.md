# Navigation View Reconstruction Ideas

> Exploratory visual / interaction notes for the 2D navigation presentation in Rusty Space.
>
> Companion to:
>
> - [`space_sailing_reactive_ship_ideas.md`](./space_sailing_reactive_ship_ideas.md)
> - [`ship_physics_implementation_notes.md`](./ship_physics_implementation_notes.md)
>
> The core idea is that the player is not looking through a literal top-down camera. They are looking at a **synthetic exterior reconstruction** generated by the ship from sensors, navigation state, prediction, and field data.

---

# 1. Why not just show a normal top-down space game?

A normal overhead rendering would be perfectly readable, but it creates a small fiction problem:

- the player is physically sitting inside a 3D ship,
- yet most of the actual piloting game occurs in a detached 2D view,
- with no explanation for where that viewpoint exists.

A literal external drone camera solves the fiction mechanically, but not elegantly. It also creates unnecessary questions about range, occlusion, FTL speed, and why this tiny camera has a better tactical picture than the ship itself.

The more useful fiction is:

> **The helm synthesizes a human-readable representation of nearby space from all available sensor data.**

There is no camera.

The view is a navigational reconstruction.

This lets the display look almost as rich and immediate as a conventional top-down space game while remaining fully diegetic.

---

# 2. The reconstruction is allowed to lie

Because the display is synthetic, it does not have to reproduce optics literally.

It can deliberately distort reality for readability:

- shrink planets,
- enlarge important ships,
- exaggerate asteroid spacing,
- flatten depth,
- simplify station geometry,
- brighten invisible fields,
- draw projected paths,
- offset overlapping contacts,
- preserve recognizable silhouettes at impossible distances,
- or render objects at different visual scales within the same frame.

The computer is not trying to make a photograph.

It is trying to make the pilot understand the situation.

This gives the visual design permission to prioritize **game feel and information density over physical correctness**.

---

# 3. Aim for "animated nautical chart," not radar

The main danger is turning the game's primary view into a sterile radar display.

Games where most interaction happens through abstract sensor screens can become visually fatiguing even when the concept is strong.

The desired middle ground is something closer to:

> **a beautiful animated nautical chart made from reconstructed 3D objects**

Possible qualities:

- ships retain chunky recognizable silhouettes,
- stations appear as simplified physical models,
- asteroids have visible shape and rotation,
- planets appear as large shaded discs or cropped bodies,
- wakes and stellar currents appear as animated ribbons or contour streams,
- trajectories appear as plotting lines,
- uncertain contacts visibly fail to resolve cleanly,
- navigational labels sit on top as instrumentation rather than replacing the world.

The display can therefore feel spatial and alive without pretending it is a normal exterior camera.

---

# 4. Reconstruction shader / resolution effect

A very short reconstruction effect can sell the conceit every time the nav computer resolves a scene or changes representation.

This should be **brief and tactile**, not a long cinematic transition.

A useful visual idea:

1. Mesh faces begin pushed outward from the object's center or local face normals.
2. Fragments rapidly collapse into their solved positions.
3. Surface shading locks in.
4. Navigation overlays and trajectory lines settle a fraction later.

Visually, it should feel like the computer assembling a perceptual model rather than loading geometry.

Possible variations:

- faces begin slightly exploded and snap inward,
- vertices briefly quantize to a coarse grid before resolving,
- wireframe confidence hulls collapse onto the final silhouette,
- false-color depth bands converge into ordinary material shading,
- object fragments resolve at slightly different times according to sensor confidence.

The effect probably wants to live in the ~100-300 ms range for routine transitions.

Longer versions could be reserved for genuinely dramatic cases such as:

- arriving in a new system,
- recovering sensors after blackout,
- first resolving an enormous unknown structure,
- or reacquiring a contact through interference.

---

# 5. Uncertainty can be visually physical

A synthetic reconstruction makes sensor uncertainty much more interesting than a simple question-mark icon.

Possible representations:

- outer faces shimmer between several possible positions,
- the silhouette intermittently collapses to lower confidence geometry,
- duplicate ghost hulls overlap slightly,
- a distant contact is represented by a coarse proxy that gains detail as confidence rises,
- occluded parts remain translucent or unstable,
- estimated velocity vectors fan into a probability cone,
- a contact's apparent size pulses within the plausible range.

This makes sensor quality visible in the reconstructed world itself.

A better sensor package can therefore change **what the pilot is able to perceive**, not merely improve a hidden accuracy stat.

---

# 6. The nav computer is a perceptual prosthesis

The stronger fiction is not simply that the computer draws a map.

It translates sensor reality into channels humans are unusually good at using:

- visual motion,
- spatial audio,
- rhythm,
- vibration,
- directional cues,
- color grouping,
- and prediction.

The ship is effectively manufacturing an artificial sensory world that lets the pilot inhabit a navigational problem far larger and stranger than human perception evolved to handle.

This helps unify:

- the 2D helm view,
- the reactive 3D interior,
- spatial audio,
- warning systems,
- and tactile ship feedback.

---

# 7. Synthetic spatial audio

A useful conceit is that the ship converts sensor events into spatially mapped audio.

Space itself does not need to make sound.

The ship deliberately creates sound because humans are extremely fast at locating and interpreting directional audio.

Examples:

- an object passing starboard produces a synthetic lateral rush from that side,
- a field shear developing aft-port produces a low directional tone behind the player's left shoulder,
- a rapidly approaching mass gains an audible presence before it dominates the display,
- a hostile contact firing from behind-right produces a sharp cue from that direction,
- a current boundary can have a spatially placed harmonic that rotates as the ship's relative orientation changes.

This is not merely atmospheric decoration.

It is another information channel.

A skilled player may eventually recognize a dangerous current or oscillation by sound before consciously inspecting the nav display.

---

# 8. Real machinery sound and synthetic navigation sound can overlap

There are two classes of audio aboard the ship:

## Physical machinery audio

Examples:

- port emitter vibration,
- capacitor relay clunk,
- cooling pump whine,
- stabilizer actuator chatter,
- hull strain,
- loose panel rattle.

These sounds originate from actual ship systems in the interior fiction.

## Synthetic situational audio

Examples:

- current direction,
- collision risk,
- external contact motion,
- closing velocity,
- gravitational capture,
- sensor uncertainty.

These are generated by the navigation computer.

The game can deliberately blur the distinction.

Experienced pilots may stop consciously distinguishing between "what the ship is physically doing" and "what the ship is telling me space is doing."

That is desirable.

The ship becomes an extension of the pilot's senses.

---

# 9. Fake proprioception for the spaceship

The interior presentation can manufacture a bodily sense of external forces without physically moving the room.

Examples:

- lateral field load biases subtle camera spring toward one side,
- console vibration increases on the side where coupling load is high,
- a projected turn causes environmental audio to rotate around the player,
- high closure rate increases the apparent urgency or density of synthetic sound,
- dangerous angular acceleration creates short directional camera and prop responses,
- stabilizer saturation creates an asymmetric low-frequency vibration before the ship visibly loses heading.

These cues should be filtered and authored.

The goal is not to reproduce raw acceleration 1:1.

The goal is to give the player's nervous system a useful intuition for what the navigation simulation is doing.

---

# 10. Do not hard-lock the ship to screen center

A center-locked camera is easy to justify as an instrument view, but it weakens the most important visual property of inertial flight:

> **heading and velocity are different things**

If the ship remains perfectly centered at all times, the player loses a strong source of motion information.

Instead, let the ship move inside a camera envelope.

A useful starting point:

```text
camera_target =
    ship_position
    + ship_velocity * velocity_lookahead
    + ship_acceleration * small_acceleration_lookahead
```

Then spring the actual camera toward that target rather than snapping to it.

Results:

- high velocity opens more screen ahead of travel,
- turning without changing velocity does not immediately reframe everything,
- the ship can visually drift within the frame,
- trajectory changes gradually pull the view around,
- momentum becomes visible even without explicit vector lines.

The ship may live within the central 40-50% of the screen rather than one fixed pixel.

---

# 11. Prefer stable world orientation over constant ship-up rotation

For inertial flight, constantly rotating the whole screen to keep the ship pointing upward may obscure the distinction between:

- orientation,
- velocity,
- and external field motion.

A stable navigational orientation is likely easier to read.

Possible defaults:

- orbital-plane north,
- local system-plane orientation,
- or a stable chart orientation selected by the nav computer.

A ship-relative mode could still exist as an optional display mode.

The important thing is that the primary view should make it easy to see:

> the ship is pointing this way but moving that way

---

# 12. Prototype three camera rules early

The camera may determine whether inertial motion feels excellent or strangely dead.

Test at least:

## A. Ship-centered

Control case.

Probably the least expressive, but useful as a baseline.

## B. Velocity-leading spring camera

The likely default.

The camera looks farther in the direction the ship is actually traveling.

## C. Predicted-trajectory camera

The camera targets a point several seconds along the currently predicted path.

This could be especially powerful in the space-sailing model because the field can bend the predicted path **before the ship has dramatically changed direction**.

Catching a planetary wake could cause the framing itself to begin curving around the body, making the external force feel large and anticipatory.

The diegetic justification is simple:

> the navigation display frames the region most relevant to the projected maneuver

---

# 13. Camera zoom should respond to the navigation problem

The reconstruction does not need a fixed physical camera distance.

The computer can frame the problem dynamically.

### Slow local flight

- closer view,
- more object detail,
- ship occupies more screen space,
- small geometry is readable.

### High-speed local flight

- pull back,
- stronger velocity lead,
- longer trajectory projection,
- more environmental context.

### Strong or chaotic field interaction

- widen framing,
- show more projected path,
- emphasize current topology.

### Interception / docking

- bias framing toward the intercept region or target,
- reduce unnecessary distant context.

Again, no literal cameraman needs to be explained because the nav system is choosing the useful representation.

---

# 14. Three visual layers may be better than a binary view

Rather than choosing between "pretty exterior" and "radar mode," use three related levels of abstraction.

## 14.1 Exterior reconstruction

Used around:

- stations,
- wrecks,
- ships,
- dense asteroid spaces,
- docking,
- local hazards.

This is the prettiest and most materially recognizable presentation.

Objects look like objects.

## 14.2 Field overlay

The same reconstructed space, but with navigational field information layered over it:

- current streamlines,
- wake contours,
- shear regions,
- projected trajectory,
- coupling state,
- danger boundaries,
- sensor uncertainty.

Players may toggle or blend this according to preference.

Experienced players may leave a partial field overlay active almost constantly.

## 14.3 Full field reconstruction

Used when moving at extreme in-system speeds or when large-scale field topology matters more than physical geometry.

Physical objects simplify into:

- mass nodes,
- field wells,
- turbulent bands,
- departure corridors,
- wake structures,
- and trajectory relationships.

The player's ship may also simplify visually.

The display becomes closer to an animated current chart.

---

# 15. FTL should be a representation change, not a different movement game

"FTL" here means absurdly fast **in-system** movement between celestial bodies, not necessarily interstellar travel.

The key rule:

> **FTL uses the same physicality and ship handling as local flight.**

The player is still:

- carrying momentum,
- orienting the ship,
- applying thrust,
- coupling to fictional currents,
- fighting stabilizer limits,
- exploiting planetary wakes,
- and dealing with ship quirks.

What changes is the **scale of the relevant information**.

At high field velocity, literal visual reconstruction becomes less useful, so the nav computer switches toward field topology.

This should not become:

> local flight game -> route-planning minigame -> local flight game

It remains one movement grammar represented differently.

---

# 16. Example local-to-FTL visual transition

A journey can naturally move through several levels of reconstruction:

```text
station exterior reconstruction
    -> local flight
    -> field overlay becomes stronger
    -> coupling rises
    -> object detail begins to simplify
    -> full field reconstruction
    -> high-speed current surfing
    -> destination topology enlarges
    -> physical contacts begin resolving again
    -> exterior reconstruction returns
    -> station approach
```

The transition can be intentionally discontinuous enough to avoid heroic camera scaling work.

This is a feature, not a failure.

The nav system is changing representation because the pilot's information needs changed.

A short reconstruction shader effect can make the mode shift feel intentional.

---

# 17. Avoid the logarithmic-camera engineering trap

There is little value in forcing one render camera and coordinate system to gracefully cover:

- docking distance,
- asteroid fields,
- moon-scale navigation,
- planetary travel,
- and stellar-scale in-system movement.

That creates large amounts of work around:

- coordinate recentering,
- precision,
- clipping,
- object scaling,
- camera damping,
- LOD,
- transitions,
- and visual continuity.

The reconstruction fiction means the game can instead use **different navigation representations over the same conceptual state**.

Smoothness matters aesthetically.

Literal geometric continuity does not.

---

# 18. Sensor equipment can alter perception

Sensors are especially interesting salvage because they can change what the player can read.

Possible sensor dimensions:

- prediction horizon,
- flow resolution,
- refresh rate,
- uncertainty,
- hidden wake detection,
- turbulence forecasting,
- object reconstruction detail,
- long-range contact classification,
- temporal lag.

Examples:

### Cheap civilian sensor package

- clean basic reconstruction,
- coarse field information,
- short prediction horizon.

### Survey package

- reveals weak currents,
- excellent field gradients,
- long environmental prediction,
- slower refresh.

### Pirate / racing sensors

- extremely fast local refresh,
- excellent close maneuver information,
- poor long-range certainty.

### Damaged sensor package

- unstable reconstruction,
- occasional geometry uncertainty,
- noisy trajectory prediction.

Better sensors should not simply make the game prettier.

Different sensor philosophies should expose different kinds of useful truth.

---

# 19. Navigation visuals can teach mastery

A novice should be able to see:

- a clear safe current,
- a recommended path,
- obvious danger zones,
- and the ship's projected trajectory.

A more experienced player begins reading finer structures:

- weak secondary wakes,
- little eddies around moons,
- temporary shear openings,
- unstable but fast routes,
- places where the autoplot is conservative.

The visual system should support a gradual shift from:

> "follow the route"

into:

> "I see another line through this"

That is the whole expressive-spaceflight goal reflected in presentation.

---

# 20. The autoplot can exist without becoming the game

A navigation computer can provide:

- safe route prediction,
- interception lines,
- expected field changes,
- and danger envelopes.

The autoplot should not eliminate player agency.

It is useful as:

- an accessibility layer,
- a baseline route,
- a way to teach current topology,
- a comparison point for expert improvisation.

A good pilot should regularly look at the safe line and think:

> I can do better than that.

---

# 21. The full field view can be visually beautiful

Do not let abstraction mean ugly.

Potential visual language:

- luminous flowing streamlines,
- contour bands around mass,
- wake ribbons curling behind planets,
- projected trajectories as elegant arcs,
- turbulent regions as broken / folding line fields,
- mass shadows as calm negative spaces,
- uncertain areas as unstable reconstruction noise,
- stars represented by dominant flow structures rather than merely icons.

The display can feel like a living scientific instrument rather than a menu.

The aim is something closer to a **dynamic weather chart crossed with a celestial navigation plot** than conventional radar.

---

# 22. Intersystem travel is a separate problem

The expressive sailing model works naturally inside a star system because there are interesting local structures:

- stellar wind,
- planets,
- moons,
- wakes,
- belts,
- stations,
- anomalies.

Between systems, there may be very little interesting navigation space.

Do not force the in-system movement model to fill an interstellar void merely for continuity.

A short space-magic transition is acceptable.

Possible fiction:

- jump gates,
- relay structures,
- stable departure regions,
- natural transition corridors,
- stellar polar exits,
- ancient infrastructure.

The important design question is not whether the jump itself is interactive.

It is whether interstellar travel preserves **locality**.

---

# 23. Why gates / departure regions may be better than a universal jump button

A jump-from-anywhere button is convenient, but tends to flatten the galaxy.

Even a graph-limited system can feel like:

```text
open map
jump
jump
jump
jump
```

Distance becomes repeated button presses rather than geography.

A fixed or semi-fixed departure region creates useful friction:

- systems have entrances and exits,
- routes toward those exits matter,
- stations can cluster around traffic corridors,
- dangerous approaches become memorable,
- local sailing participates in interstellar travel.

The gate or departure corridor acts as a **topological chokepoint**.

This gives the star system shape.

---

# 24. Departure infrastructure can use the same sailing grammar

Do not invent a separate gate minigame.

The departure structure can create a strong stable flow that the player merges into using the same movement system.

Examples:

### Commercial gate

- broad forgiving outbound current,
- easy alignment,
- strong automation support.

### Broken relay

- irregular coupling,
- unstable entry line,
- requires manual correction.

### Frontier natural corridor

- weak but usable transition current,
- dangerous local field interactions,
- popular with smugglers or expert pilots.

This keeps interstellar departure connected to the core handling model.

---

# 25. The intersystem jump should probably be punctuation

Once the player reaches the departure geometry, the actual interstellar transition can be short and spectacular.

There is no need to turn hyperspace itself into a repeated gameplay challenge.

A useful rhythm:

```text
navigate toward departure region
    -> merge into transition flow
    -> field locks
    -> brief reality-breaking jump effect
    -> arrive in destination system entry region
    -> reconstruction resolves
```

The jump remains exciting because it is punctuation at the end of meaningful navigation rather than a repeated low-information interaction.

The real enemy is not loading screens.

It is **repeated interaction with no interesting decisions**.

---

# 26. Reconstruction on system arrival

Arriving in a new system is a perfect place to use a stronger version of the reconstruction effect.

Possible sequence:

1. Hyperspace / transition effect ends abruptly.
2. Display is mostly unresolved field noise.
3. Major stellar mass appears first.
4. Planetary bodies resolve as coarse nodes.
5. Known navigation infrastructure appears.
6. Nearby contacts reconstruct.
7. Physical local geometry resolves only as useful.

This can make entering a system feel like the ship is **building reality back up around the pilot**.

It also provides a clean loading / streaming fiction if needed.

---

# 27. Visual style can belong to the ship

Because the reconstruction is software-generated, different ship systems or nav computers can have distinct presentation personalities.

Possible differences:

- reconstruction resolution,
- interpolation smoothness,
- temporal ghosting,
- overlay density,
- typography,
- object simplification style,
- field visualization style,
- prediction confidence display.

Be careful not to make expensive equipment simply mean "higher graphical fidelity."

A battered old display can be visually wonderful in its own way.

A sophisticated system may actually be more restrained because it has better confidence about what information matters.

---

# 28. Prototype order

The prototype should begin visually simple but preserve the architecture needed for these ideas.

## Prototype A: plain top-down reconstruction

Render:

- player ship,
- one planet / mass source,
- one or two obstacles,
- trajectory line,
- current vectors.

Use the real camera behavior under test.

No elaborate shader work yet.

## Prototype B: camera language

Implement and compare:

- center lock,
- velocity lead,
- trajectory lead,
- dynamic zoom.

Choose the framing that best communicates inertia.

## Prototype C: field overlay

Add:

- current streamlines,
- wake visualization,
- coupling state,
- projected trajectory curvature.

Test whether the player can understand why the ship is being pushed.

## Prototype D: reconstruction effect

Add the short exploded-face / resolving-geometry transition.

Use it for:

- mode entry,
- contact acquisition,
- sensor recovery.

Keep it fast enough that it remains punctuation rather than UI friction.

## Prototype E: full field representation

Create a second representation of the same navigation state for high-speed travel.

Prove that:

- ship handling remains identical in principle,
- the player still feels momentum,
- field topology becomes easier to read,
- the mode shift does not feel like switching to a disconnected minigame.

## Prototype F: synthetic audio

Map a few external events into directional sound:

- current direction,
- high closure rate,
- incoming contact,
- field shear.

Test whether players can begin identifying events without staring at UI.

---

# 29. Useful debug views

Build these before polishing the art:

- literal world-space positions,
- reconstructed display positions,
- actual velocity vector,
- camera target,
- camera spring state,
- predicted trajectory,
- current field samples,
- object confidence / uncertainty,
- abstraction level,
- sensor range / resolution,
- audio cue positions.

The visual system is intentionally lying for readability, so it needs tools that reveal **how it is lying**.

---

# 30. Acceptance criteria

The navigation presentation is on the right track when:

- the player understands momentum without relying only on vector arrows,
- the ship can visibly move one direction while facing another,
- the camera helps rather than erases that distinction,
- the view feels like part of the ship rather than a detached game camera,
- long play sessions do not feel like staring at radar,
- field information is readable without drowning physical objects,
- high-speed mode still feels like flying the same ship,
- local and field representations can change without heroic continuous scaling,
- sensor uncertainty is visually meaningful,
- interior sound / motion cues reinforce rather than duplicate the display,
- and the player can eventually read navigational situations through several sensory channels at once.

---

# 31. Concise presentation thesis

```text
SENSORS + NAVIGATION STATE
        -> synthetic world reconstruction
        -> readable object geometry
        -> field visualization
        -> predictive camera framing
        -> uncertainty rendering
        -> synthetic spatial audio
        -> reactive interior cues
```

The player is not watching their ship from outside.

They are sitting inside a machine that is continuously translating space into a perceptual language a human pilot can actually use.

That fiction gives the game permission to be readable, beautiful, abstract, and tactile at the same time.
